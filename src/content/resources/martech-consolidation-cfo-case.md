Marketing knows the stack has too many tools. Finance sees a list of subscriptions and a request for time to change them, with no clear figure for what comes back or when. So the plan sits in a shared folder until the next renewal goes through on its own.

Budgets are making this conversation harder to postpone. The 2026 Gartner CMO Spend Survey of 401 CMOs, reported by [Chief Marketer](https://www.chiefmarketer.com/gartner-cmo-spend-survey-budgets-reflect-increase-in-consumption-based-martech-paid-media-spend/), found martech's share of marketing budgets at a five-year low of 19.4 percent, down from 26.6 percent in 2021. A CFO looking at 2027 numbers will want evidence before approving any change to that line.

## Quick answer: how do you make the case to your CFO for consolidating your martech stack?

- Show the full cost of each tool, including the people and integrations around it.
- Build the savings around actual renewal dates, so finance can see when cash returns.
- Count one-off migration costs and put them in the same table as the savings.
- Name the operational risks and the owner of each one.
- Ask for approval on a first phase with a review date.

## What a CFO needs to see

A CFO needs to see three things on one page: how much cash comes back, when it comes back, and what could go wrong on the way. Efficiency arguments and complaints about tool overload rarely move a budget. A dated table with a named owner gives finance something it can test and approve.

That changes how you write the proposal. Lead with the number and the timing, then explain the marketing benefit underneath it. Your CFO will read the first table closely and skim the rest, so anything that matters to the decision belongs there.

Check the numbers with someone in finance before the meeting. An hour with a finance analyst will catch the assumptions that would otherwise get picked apart in the room, and it gives you an ally when the proposal is discussed.

## Why subscription savings alone won't carry it

Subscription savings are the easiest number to find and the easiest one to challenge. Finance knows that cancelled licences often come back as new purchases, and that the work a tool did has to go somewhere. A credible case shows the full running cost of each tool and what replaces it.

Usage data makes the strongest opening. Zylo's [2024 index](https://zylo.com/news/2024-saas-management-index) found companies were using only 49 percent of their provisioned licences. Your own figure matters more, so pull last-login and seat data for every tool you plan to touch.

Then add the costs that never appear on the invoice: integrations someone maintains, agency hours spent working around a tool, and the time your team spends reconciling data between platforms. Those numbers are estimates, so label them as estimates and show how you reached them.

Pricing is shifting underneath all of this. In the same [Gartner survey](https://www.chiefmarketer.com/gartner-cmo-spend-survey-budgets-reflect-increase-in-consumption-based-martech-paid-media-spend/), 56 percent of respondents had increased the share of their martech budget going to consumption-based pricing. Any tool billed on usage needs a low and a high estimate in your model, because its cost can move during the year and finance will want to see how far.

## How to build the case, step by step

The case comes together in five steps, and most of the work uses data you already have. You start from an inventory, attach real costs and renewal dates, net off the one-off work, assign the risks, and then ask for a single first phase. A small team can finish it within a fortnight.

1. Build a complete inventory of every marketing tool, with its owner, cost, seats, last-login data and renewal date.
2. Calculate the full annual cost of each tool you plan to remove, including integrations and the staff time around it.
3. Place each saving in the month it actually lands, based on the contract's renewal and notice dates.
4. Subtract one-off costs such as data migration, retraining and any overlap period when two tools run together.
5. Ask finance to approve a first phase covering the safest cuts, with a review date before the second phase is released.

If you have no inventory yet, a one-day [stack audit](/insights/martech-stack-audit) produces it. Step 3 is the one marketing teams most often skip, and it is the first thing a CFO will check.

## Why renewal dates decide the timeline

Renewal dates decide when savings become real, so they should set the order of your plan. A tool on an annual contract that renewed last month saves nothing for almost a year, however little it is used. A tool with a notice period closing soon may need a decision this quarter.

Map every contract on a single timeline covering the next 12 months. Mark the notice deadline as well as the renewal date, since the notice period is when you lose the option to leave.

Lay the savings over that timeline and you have a cash view a CFO can drop into a forecast. It also tells you which tools to tackle first, a question that stalls many [consolidation](/insights/martech-stack-consolidation) plans.

## The risks finance will ask about

Finance will ask what breaks if a tool goes, and the proposal should answer before anyone raises it. List each dependency, who owns the migration, and what happens to the data. A short risk table shows you have thought past the saving and makes the approval easier to give.

The warning signs are well documented. Writing in [CIO](https://www.cio.com/article/4132844/why-saas-cost-optimization-is-an-operating-model-problem-not-a-budget-exercise.html), Pawan Deep Singh describes a software cost-cutting programme where teams ended up rebuilding lost functionality manually and quietly adopting unapproved tools. The productivity damage never showed up in the spend reports.

His advice is to map ownership, usage and dependency before touching contracts. Add data retention to that list, because contract end dates can cut off access to historical campaign data that attribution and reporting still rely on.

## What to ask for in the meeting

Ask for one decision: approval of a first phase with a defined saving, a named owner and a review date. A small, well-scoped request is easy to say yes to, and delivering it earns you the credibility the larger phases will need. Leave the full programme as an outline on the final page.

Bring the plan for keeping the savings as well. CFOs have seen software costs creep back after clean-up projects, and a simple [request process](/insights/martech-tool-request-process) for new tools answers that concern directly.
