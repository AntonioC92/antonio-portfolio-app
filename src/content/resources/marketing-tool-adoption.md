The kickoff call happens, the Slack announcement goes out, and the dashboards get built. Three months later, half the team is back in spreadsheets, and the tool you paid for is running at a fraction of what it was built to do. Training is part of the story. The bigger issue is sequencing, and the data says this plays out almost everywhere, across teams and tools of every size.

## Quick answer: why doesn't a new marketing tool get used?

- Utilization has been dropping for years. Gartner's 2022 survey of 324 marketers found utilization at 42% of stack capabilities, down from 58% two years earlier, even as CMOs put a quarter of their marketing budget into the technology itself, per [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2022-10-03-gartner-survey-finds-marketers-utilize-just-42-percent-of-their-martech-stack-capabilities).
- Integration gaps drive most of the resistance. A Content Marketing Institute and MarketingProfs survey of 925 B2B marketers found only 28% say they have the technology they actually need, and 48% name integrating data across platforms as their biggest challenge, per [CMI research](https://martech.org/only-28-of-b2b-content-marketers-report-having-the-technology-they-need/).
- Resistance follows a predictable pattern: unclear value, extra friction, misaligned incentives, no single owner, and low trust in the data the tool produces.
- Fixing adoption takes a sequence spread over weeks. Simplifying the process before digitizing it, then reinforcing the habit for 60 to 90 days, moves usage further than a better onboarding deck does.
- AI features make this sharper right now. Gartner's 2025 survey of 413 martech leaders found 45% say vendor-supplied AI agents underperform expectations, and half say their stack isn't ready to support one, per [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-10-29-gartner-survey-finds-45-percent-of-martech-leaders-say-existing-vendor-offered-ai-agents-fail-to-meet-their-expectations-of-promised-business-performance).

## Where the usage actually goes

Most new marketing tools end up used by one or two people and ignored by everyone else on the team. Gartner's research puts martech utilization at 42% of stack capabilities, a drop from 58% two years before, even as the same survey found CMOs allocating around a quarter of their marketing budget to the stack itself.

That gap between spend and use shows up across other research too. Emarketer's analysis of martech spend found 47% of decision-makers name stack complexity and poor system integration as their top hurdle, with another 34% pointing to underskilled teams as the barrier to getting full value from what they already bought.

A [stack audit](/insights/martech-stack-audit) before rollout catches a good share of this. It surfaces the tools that overlap, the ones nobody asked for, and the two or three real gaps worth filling, before you add one more license to the pile.

## Why teams go back to the old way

Teams revert to spreadsheets when a new tool doesn't tie to something they already care about, like fewer reworks or faster approvals, or when it adds steps without removing any. Unclear value, added friction, and no single owner are the three reasons that show up most often.

The Content Marketing Institute's research backs this up from a different angle: 48% of B2B marketers call integrating or correlating data across platforms their biggest challenge, which is exactly the friction a new tool is supposed to remove and often doesn't. When it fails to, the old spreadsheet stays open in another tab.

Low trust compounds the problem. When a dashboard doesn't match what a rep already knows is true, the rep defaults to the version they trust, and the new tool quietly becomes a reporting layer nobody checks. Ambiguous ownership is usually the root cause: nobody is accountable for definitions, approvals, or data quality, which is the same gap that leaves [marketing ops](/insights/marketing-ops-underfunded) chronically underfunded in the first place.

## The sequence that actually sticks

Adoption sticks when it follows a sequence instead of a single launch event: diagnose where the real friction sits, simplify the process before you digitize it, enable the team with actual training, then reinforce the habit for 60 to 90 days. Skipping straight to rollout is the most common mistake teams make.

[Pedowitz Group](https://www.pedowitzgroup.com/why-wont-teams-adopt-new-digital-processes) frames this as a Digital Process Adoption Maturity Matrix: six capabilities, process clarity, tool experience, automation, enablement, data governance, and measurement, each with a named owner and a single KPI. Low maturity on any one of those six is usually enough to stall the whole rollout, even when the tool itself works fine.

Running a [readiness checklist](/insights/marketing-automation-readiness-checklist) before rollout forces the simplify step. You remove the steps that don't earn their place before you ask anyone to learn a new interface, which is the opposite order most vendors push.

## Proving the case before you roll anything out

A new tool earns buy-in faster with time data than with a features list. Track how long the current process actually takes, compare it against the new workflow, and bring that time gap into the room where the decision gets made.

The stakes for skipping this step are real. BCG found 70% of digital transformations fall short of their stated objectives, and Lemon Learning found 66% of change management projects run late, over budget, or short of planned functionality, both cited in [CoSchedule](https://coschedule.com/blog/marketing-team-new-tools)'s breakdown of what actually moves a team to adopt something new.

Randstad's research, in the [same breakdown](https://coschedule.com/blog/marketing-team-new-tools), adds the piece most rollouts skip: 58% of employees say their employer has advanced digital tools but never trained them to use it. A simple time log, task, time spent, person, repeated five to ten times before and after the change, usually makes a stronger case than anything in a vendor's sales deck, and it costs nothing but an afternoon to build.

## What this means for AI tools specifically

AI features sharpen the adoption problem instead of solving it. Gartner's 2025 survey of 413 martech leaders found 45% say vendor-supplied AI agents underperform what was promised, and half say their own data and systems aren't ready to support one at all.

The fix is the same sequence as above, applied earlier. Keep [quality checkpoints](/insights/ai-content-workflow-quality-control) where a person reviews AI output before it reaches a client or a live campaign, and expand its scope only once the team actually trusts what it produces. Buying the agent before the stack is ready just adds a sixth capability to the maturity matrix that nobody owns.

A rollout plan that accounts for sequencing, training, and reinforcement from day one costs far less than fixing an ignored tool six months in.
