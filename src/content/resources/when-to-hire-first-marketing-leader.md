Most founders do not decide to hire marketing leadership. They arrive at it, usually a few months after the founder has quietly become the head of marketing, sales enablement, and content, on top of everything else. By the time the question gets asked out loud, the business has usually outgrown the improvised version already.

The harder question is not whether to bring in marketing leadership. It is when, and in what form. Fractional CMO, first full-time hire, or agency retainer are three very different commitments with different costs, different ramp times, and different failure modes if you pick wrong for your stage.

## Quick answer: when should you hire your first marketing leader?

- **Founder bandwidth is the real constraint.** If marketing decisions are getting made in spare hours between customer calls, the business is already paying an opportunity cost.
- **You have real signal to work with.** A handful of paying customers you did not personally know beforehand carries more weight than a strong hunch about the market.
- **Sales is about to outgrow one person selling.** Gearing up to hire a first salesperson or push into a new channel is a common trigger point, per [Vendep](https://www.vendep.com/post/nailing-your-first-marketing-hire-in-b2b-saas).
- **Fractional beats full-time before you can define the role precisely.** A fractional CMO lets you buy senior judgment before you commit to a permanent org design.
- **Do not hire before you have a working ICP.** Bringing in marketing leadership to fix an unclear product or audience just adds a salary to an unsolved problem.

## The signals that actually mean it is time

Founders tend to wait for a revenue number that never arrives cleanly. In practice, the more reliable signals are behavioural, not financial. [Vendep](https://www.vendep.com/post/nailing-your-first-marketing-hire-in-b2b-saas) points to three: paying customers outside the founder's personal network, a sales function that is about to scale, and a founder whose calendar has no room left for marketing at all. Post-seed or early Series A is the typical window, but the stage label matters less than whether those three signals are present.

The inverse is just as useful. If you are still pre-revenue, still pivoting the offer every few weeks, or still unclear on who the ideal customer actually is, marketing leadership will not fix that. It will just add payroll to a positioning problem. Get the [martech stack](/insights/martech-stack-audit) and the basic tracking in order first, so whoever you bring in has something real to work from on day one.

There is also a quieter cost worth naming directly: founder time. Every week a founder spends writing ad copy, chasing a content calendar, or debugging a landing page is a week not spent on product, sales, or fundraising, the things only the founder can do. That opportunity cost rarely shows up on a spreadsheet, which is exactly why it gets ignored for too long. By the time it becomes visible in slipping deal velocity or a stalled roadmap, the business has usually been paying it for months.

## Fractional, full-time, or agency: what "hiring" actually means here

These three options solve different problems, and founders often reach for the wrong one because the pricing is the first thing they compare.

A fractional CMO gives you senior strategic judgment for a fraction of the commitment. According to [GoFractional](https://www.gofractional.com/blog/fractional-cmo-salary), monthly retainers typically run from $4,000 to $20,000, with $12,000 a common average for an experienced operator working a partial week. That buys someone who has built and broken marketing systems before, without a 12-month severance risk if the fit is wrong. The role itself has also changed shape in recent years, shifting from pure strategy advice toward hands-on system building, a shift we covered in more depth in [our piece](/insights/future-fractional-cmo-operating-builder) on where the role is heading.

A full-time CMO makes sense once the marketing function has enough scope, budget, and headcount to justify a permanent seat at the leadership table. [Payscale](https://www.payscale.com/research/US/Job=Chief_Marketing_Officer_%28CMO%29/Salary) puts average CMO base salary in the US at $190,913 a year, before equity, bonus, or the team they will need to hire underneath them. That is a serious commitment to make on the strength of a hunch.

An agency retainer buys execution capacity, not leadership. It is a reasonable choice when the strategy is already set and you need hands to run campaigns, but it rarely replaces the person who should be deciding what to run and why. [Digital Applied](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/fractional-cmo-2026-when-it-beats-full-time-hire-decision-matrix) frames this as a decision matrix rather than a ladder: the right choice depends on whether you are missing strategy, execution, or both.

## What to have ready before you hire anyone

Bringing in marketing leadership too early usually looks the same, regardless of which of the three you pick. The new hire spends the first month building basic infrastructure that should have existed already: a CRM that reflects reality, a shared definition of a qualified lead, and reporting that leadership actually trusts. None of that requires a CMO. It requires the founder or an ops-minded generalist to spend a week getting the [marketing system](/insights/modern-marketing-system-2025) into a state where someone senior can walk in and be useful immediately.

Run a quick check before the search starts. Is the ICP written down anywhere, or does it live only in the founder's head? Is there a CRM with real pipeline stages, or a spreadsheet standing in for one? Can anyone answer, without guessing, which channel produced last month's best customers? If the honest answer to more than one of those is no, spend a few weeks closing that gap before the first interview, using something like our [readiness checklist](/insights/marketing-automation-readiness-checklist) as a starting point.

## The first 90 days matter more than the title

Whichever option you choose, the first quarter decides whether the hire sticks. Give a fractional CMO or new full-time leader a narrow, measurable mandate for the first 90 days rather than a broad "fix marketing" brief. A tight audit of what is working, what is being tracked, and what the biggest single lever is tends to build more trust than an ambitious plan with no evidence behind it yet.

This is also where the choice between fractional and full-time often resolves itself naturally. If the 90-day engagement produces a clear operating model and the business has grown enough to need someone in the seat full-time, the fractional arrangement becomes the audition for a permanent hire. If it produces a lean, well-run system that a fractional leader can keep steering for a few hours a week, there may be no need to convert it into a full-time role at all. Either outcome is a good one, and neither is a step backward from where the business started.
