Most marketing teams now check whether ChatGPT or Perplexity cites their own site. Almost none check what those same tools cite when a competitor comes up in the same conversation. That gap has a real cost: if a rival's blog post or a directory listing keeps getting pulled in as the source on comparison questions, you lose consideration before your own content gets a chance to compete.

## Quick answer: how do you track which sources AI cites for competitors?

- Run the same buyer-style prompts you use for your own visibility tracking, but log the citations for every brand the answer names, competitors included.
- Use a dedicated AI monitoring tool that reports cited domains and source pages alongside brand mentions.
- Build a set of 15 to 20 prompts across comparison, best-of, and problem-first phrasing, since competitors surface differently depending on how a question is asked.
- Log which domains get cited repeatedly: competitors' own sites, review platforms, directories, or trade publications.
- Treat a recurring competitor citation as a content gap. If a directory page outranks you as the cited source, that's a specific page to build or pursue placement on.

## Why tracking your own citations only shows half the picture

Tracking your own AI citations tells you whether you're showing up. It leaves out who's winning the citation instead, and why a rival's page keeps getting pulled into the same comparison answer. The competitive read only appears once you widen the same prompt set to cover every brand the answer names.

We've written before about the [difference](/insights/ai-citations-vs-mentions) between being named and being linked as a source. That distinction applies to competitors too. A rival might get mentioned constantly without ever being cited, while a smaller competitor earns fewer mentions but owns the one page every model keeps linking back to. That second pattern is the genuine content gap worth closing.

Running your own [visibility tracking](/insights/tracking-ai-search-visibility) and a competitor citation audit off the same prompt set keeps the comparison honest. Different prompt sets produce different answers, so mixing sources makes the gap look bigger or smaller than it actually is.

Picture a marketing automation vendor whose own visibility tracking shows solid mention rates for "best marketing automation platform for small teams." Widening that same prompt to log every citation shows a review aggregator winning the linked source almost every time, for every brand in the answer, including theirs. That points to a placement fix on the aggregator rather than a content fix on their own site, and the two call for different work entirely.

## What a competitor citation audit actually involves

A competitor citation audit means running a fixed prompt set through a monitoring tool and logging, for every brand the answer names, which page it linked as the source. The output worth keeping is a table of domains by prompt, mapped against every brand that showed up in that answer.

Build the prompt set across three phrasings, since competitors surface differently depending on how a question gets asked. Comparison prompts ("X versus Y") name direct rivals by default. Best-of prompts ("best tool for Z") surface a wider field, often five or six brands per answer. Problem-first prompts ("how do I fix Z") skip brand names entirely at first and reward whichever page frames the fix most directly, which is often where the real content gap hides.

Ahrefs Brand Radar now processes roughly [370 million prompts a month](https://ahrefs.com/blog/brand-radar-methodology/) across ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, and Gemini, which gives a sense of how much answer volume this category already covers. Google's AI Overviews alone accounts for the largest share of that query volume, ahead of ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Copilot combined.

For a smaller budget, [Otterly.ai](https://help.otterly.ai/pricing-of-otterlyai) starts at a lite tier built around 15 tracked prompts, which happens to match the size of prompt set most teams need for a first competitor pass. Scale up once the first audit shows which prompts are worth tracking every week versus which ones rarely shift.

## Reading the source list: brand sites versus third parties

The source list from a competitor audit splits into two categories that need different responses. A competitor's own domain getting cited means their page answers the question better right now. A third-party domain getting cited, a review site, a directory, a trade publication, means the opportunity sits outside anyone's own site entirely.

That second category is often the bigger one. Answering how AI models actually [pick a source](/insights/how-ai-search-engines-decide-which-sites-to-cite) explains why: models favor pages that answer a question directly and cleanly, and a well-structured directory entry frequently beats a vaguer page on a brand's own domain.

One marketer described the core problem well in a [Quora thread](https://www.quora.com/How-do-you-track-whether-your-content-is-being-cited-by-ChatGPT-or-Perplexity) on tracking AI citations: these platforms operate close to a black box, with no analytics trail comparable to a search console. The workaround people land on is manual, typing the same prompts repeatedly and logging what comes back by hand. A monitoring tool exists specifically to replace that manual loop at scale.

Watch for one pattern inside the source list: the same third-party domain winning the citation across several unrelated brands in the same answer. That usually means the model has learned to trust that domain as a category reference, independent of which brand it's discussing. Competing for a spot on that one page will do more for citation share than publishing five new pages of your own.

## Turning a citation gap into a plan

A citation gap becomes actionable once it's sorted into two buckets: pages to build, and placements to pursue. If a competitor's own page keeps winning the citation, the fix is building a page that answers the same question more directly, following the same [structure](/insights/structuring-pages-for-ai-citations) that gets pages cited in the first place.

If a third-party page keeps winning instead, the fix runs through outreach rather than content. Getting a stronger entry on that directory, review site, or comparison roundup, and making sure it accurately represents the current offer, closes the gap faster than adding another page to a domain the model isn't citing anyway.

Whichever bucket applies, the same [measurement rhythm](/insights/measuring-ai-overviews-citation-value) used for tracking a brand's own citation depth works here too. Log the fix, then rerun the same prompt set a month later to see whether the citation actually moved.

## How often to rerun the audit

A competitor citation audit needs a set schedule to stay useful, since a one-off report goes stale within weeks. Model updates reshuffle citations without warning, and a competitor who wins a particular prompt this month can lose it entirely after the next release with no change in their own content.

Monthly is the right cadence for the full prompt set, since that matches how often most teams already review AI visibility reporting internally. A smaller weekly check on the five or six highest-intent prompts catches a shift early enough to act on it before a full quarter passes.
