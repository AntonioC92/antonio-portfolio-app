Most companies vet a fractional marketing leader the same way they'd vet a full-time hire: a resume review, a couple of culture-fit calls, then an offer. That approach misses the questions that actually determine whether the engagement works. A fractional arrangement runs on a contract, a defined scope, and a handful of assumptions about access and cadence, and if those aren't nailed down before signing, the mismatch shows up in month two as missed deliverables or a scope fight nobody wants to have.

The [fractional CMO market](https://www.sci-tech-today.com/stats/fractional-executive-hiring-statistics/) has grown fast enough that vetting hasn't caught up. Global fractional executive spend hit an estimated $5.7 billion in 2025 and is growing at roughly 14% a year, and by the end of 2026 more than 40% of US SMEs are projected to use some form of fractional leadership. Supply has kept pace too: LinkedIn profiles listing a fractional executive title jumped from around 2,000 in 2022 to 110,000 in 2024. More candidates and more buyers means more variance in quality, and a checklist is the fastest way to close that gap before you're locked into a bad fit.

## Quick answer: what should you ask before hiring a fractional CMO?

- What exactly is in scope, and what is explicitly excluded, in writing.
- What will you see, how often, and in what format.
- What triggers termination, and what happens to work product and access when it does.
- How do they make decisions when the data is incomplete or conflicting.
- What would this role cost as a full-time hire, and does the fractional math actually beat it.

## Define the scope before the job title

A title tells you almost nothing about what a fractional CMO will actually do day to day. Two candidates with the same title can mean strategic advisory calls once a month, or hands-on ownership of campaigns, hiring, and vendor management.

Ask for a written responsibility breakdown before you talk price. [Common contract mistakes](https://fractionus.com/blog/contract-terms-founders-get-wrong-hiring-fractional) in fractional hiring start with vague phrases like "provide strategic leadership," which sound reassuring and commit to nothing. Push for a simple matrix marking each function, brand strategy, demand generation, hiring, reporting, as "lead," "support," or "excluded," so there's no ambiguity about who owns what once the engagement starts.

This also protects the candidate. A good fractional operator wants the boundaries clear too, since scope creep on a part-time retainer is how burnout and resentment start on both sides.

## Ask exactly what you'll see, and when

Reporting cadence is where fractional engagements quietly fail. You're paying for judgment you won't observe firsthand most of the week, so the reporting rhythm is your actual visibility into whether the work is happening.

Ask what a typical week or month looks like in terms of check-ins, and get specific: a monthly strategy call plus a written priority memo is a real answer, "regular updates" is not. Deliverables should carry [explicit format and acceptance criteria](https://yanivgoldenberg.com/fractional-cmo-contract/): a named owner, a completion date, and a window for you to review and either accept or flag it as deficient. Without that structure, "the strategy is in progress" can describe both a productive month and a stalled one.

If the engagement includes hands-on execution work, ask how they'll interface with your existing team. A fractional CMO who can't articulate how they'll hand off work to your in-house marketer or [audit your existing stack](/insights/martech-stack-audit) before making changes is planning to work in isolation, which rarely goes well.

## Get the exit terms in writing before you need them

Nobody wants to think about how an engagement ends while they're still trying to start it, but this is exactly the term most contracts get wrong. Standard 30-day consulting exit clauses, borrowed from generic freelance agreements, don't account for how much institutional knowledge a fractional CMO accumulates.

Ask specifically about notice period, and push for 90 days with milestone-based exceptions rather than a flat 30. Also ask what happens to campaign access, vendor relationships, and documentation on exit: does the contract require a transition period where the outgoing leader assists onboarding a replacement, or does the relationship just stop.

The same applies to intellectual property. Company-specific work product, your campaign data, your positioning docs, should belong to you outright. General frameworks and methodologies the CMO brought with them, and will use with other clients, shouldn't be something you try to claim ownership of. Trying to grab both is a fast way to lose strong candidates during negotiation.

## Test for judgment under pressure

A resume tells you what industries someone has worked in. It doesn't tell you how they think when the data is messy or contradictory, which is the actual job most of the time. Ask a candidate to walk through a real decision they made with incomplete information: a channel they cut before the data was conclusive, a budget reallocation they made under pressure.

Watch for whether they defend the decision on outcome alone or can explain the reasoning that held up regardless of how it turned out. That distinction matters more for a fractional hire than a full-time one, because you'll see far less of their process day to day and need to trust the judgment behind it. If you're also weighing a [full-time marketing manager against a fractional CMO](/insights/marketing-manager-vs-fractional-cmo), this is usually the deciding factor: a full-time hire you can coach over time, a fractional one you're trusting from week one.

Ask for a specific example of a wrong call they made alongside the wins they'll volunteer freely. Anyone who claims a perfect track record either hasn't done enough real work or isn't being straight with you.

## Compare the ROI math to a full-time hire

Fractional only makes sense if the math actually works out for your stage, and it's worth running the numbers before falling for the pitch. A [full-time CMO](https://www.sci-tech-today.com/stats/fractional-executive-hiring-statistics/) typically runs $275,000 to $500,000 or more in total comp, while fractional engagements land between $60,000 and $240,000 annually depending on scope and hours, a 40% to 70% reduction at comparable seniority.

Speed to value differs too. Fractional leaders are generally expected to show measurable direction within 30 to 45 days, against six to nine months for a traditional executive hire once you count search time and ramp-up. That gap is real, but it only pays off once the scope and reporting cadence questions above are already answered. Skip those and the lower price tag just buys you less clarity at the same output level.

Ask directly what results they expect to show in the first 60 days, and how that compares to what a [first month typically looks like](/insights/fractional-cmo-month-one) in a well-run engagement. If they can't give you a concrete answer, that's diagnostic on its own.

Once scope, reporting, exit terms, and ROI are settled, the vetting conversation becomes a real negotiation instead of a leap of faith.
