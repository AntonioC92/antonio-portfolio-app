Budget season starts the same way at most small marketing teams. Someone opens last year's spreadsheet, adds a percentage, and calls it a plan. A bad quarter breaks that plan fast. So does a platform change, or a founder asking why spend keeps climbing with no clearer return attached.

2027 planning is already underway, and the data behind it points somewhere specific. Budgets are growing again after a cautious year. A bigger share of that money is earmarked for AI, and most teams are not actually ready to spend it well.

## Quick answer: how should a small marketing team build a 2027 budget?

- Start from customer acquisition cost and payback period, instead of a percentage-of-revenue rule carried over from last year.
- Treat AI spend as a separate line with its own readiness check, since intent to use it is running well ahead of the ability to use it.
- Treat planning season as the one structured moment each year built for cutting something, alongside everything you plan to add.
- Reserve a small, explicit line for visibility in AI search and answer engines, separate from paid media.
- Build the budget as a case you can defend with real numbers attached, start to finish.

## Why most 2027 budgets start as last year's number with a markup

Incremental budgeting feels safe because it avoids a fight, but it quietly locks in whatever mistakes already exist in the mix. [Forrester's](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/budget-planning-2027-b2b-marketing-leaders-must-prioritize-focus-for-resilience/) 2027 B2B guidance argues that adjusting prior allocations and expanding programs in parallel reinforces legacy models and spreads investment too thin.

The fix isn't a bigger number. It's picking a smaller number of things to fund properly and being willing to say no to the rest, which is a harder conversation than copying last year's row totals forward.

That conversation only happens once a year for most teams, around planning season, so it's worth treating as a real review rather than a formality on the way to the real work.

## What the data says about where marketing budgets are heading

Budgets are rising again after a year most teams spent defending what they already had. [Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/press-newsroom/forresters-2027-budget-planning-guides/) found 89% of B2B marketing decision-makers and 91% of B2C marketers expect their marketing investment to increase over the next 12 months, based on a survey of more than 2,600 decision-makers.

[Gartner's](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-05-11-gartner-2026-cmo-spend-survey-finds-cmos-allocate-15-point-3-percent-of-marketing-budgets-to-ai-but-only-30-percent-are-ready-to-scale-ai-capabilities) 2026 CMO Spend Survey puts average marketing spend at 7.8% of company revenue, up slightly from 7.7% the year before. That survey leans enterprise, with most respondents at companies over $1 billion in revenue across North America, the UK, and Europe, so treat the percentage as a directional anchor rather than a target for a smaller business.

## Where AI spend fits, and why readiness lags intent

Every CMO wants an AI line item this year. Far fewer have the team or the process to make that spend pay back, which is the gap that should shape how much of a 2027 budget actually goes toward it.

Gartner's same survey found CMOs allocate an average of 15.3% of their marketing budget to AI initiatives, yet only 30% describe their organization as having mature readiness to scale AI capabilities. [MarketingDive's](https://www.marketingdive.com/news/ai-remains-a-top-priority-for-cmos-but-spending-lags-gartner/819806/) coverage of the same data notes that 70% of CMOs call AI leadership a critical 2026 goal, a wide gap between ambition and capability.

Organizations with genuinely mature AI readiness allocated 21.3% of their budget to AI and ran larger overall budgets too, at 8.9% of revenue against the 7.8% survey average. That pattern suggests AI spend pays off once the groundwork exists first, and that groundwork is usually clean, connected data rather than a new tool.

Readiness, in practice, is a reporting problem before it's a tooling one. Our own breakdown of [AI reporting](/insights/ai-marketing-reporting) workflows is a reasonable gut check for whether that groundwork is there yet, or whether the AI line item in a 2027 plan would just be funding a guess dressed up as a pilot project.

## What to cut before you add anything new

A budget conversation that only adds line items never gets to the honest question, which is what has stopped earning its spot. Forrester's 2027 guidance treats divestment as a growth lever in its own right, pointing specifically at eliminating underperforming segments and stopping AI initiatives that were started before the team was ready for them.

That's a useful forcing function even outside AI. Before adding a new channel or tool to the 2027 plan, run the same [stack audit](/insights/martech-stack-audit) you'd run on software against your media mix.

Anything that only gets funded out of habit is the easiest money to redirect. A channel that hasn't been reviewed properly in two budget cycles is usually still there because nobody owns the decision to cut it, whatever the numbers actually show.

## Building the number around acquisition cost

A percentage-of-revenue rule is only a starting point. We've covered the [benchmark data](/insights/marketing-budget-percentage-of-revenue) behind that rule elsewhere. What matters for 2027 is the number underneath it: what a customer actually costs to acquire, and what that customer is worth once they convert.

[WebFX's](https://www.webfx.com/blog/marketing/how-to-plan-a-marketing-budget/) planning framework builds the number from goals and sales cycle length outward, rather than starting from a top-line percentage and working backward. That's the right order for a small team.

Decide what growth needs to happen first. Price out what it actually costs to make that happen, across every channel involved, then check the resulting number against a benchmark like Gartner's instead of starting from one. Teams that feel chronically under-resourced on [marketing ops](/insights/marketing-ops-underfunded) often find the real constraint sitting there, well upstream of the headline budget line.

## How to defend the budget before it gets approved

A budget survives review when it reads like a case. [Liftlab's](https://liftlab.com/blog/marketing-budget-planning-2027/) planning guidance frames this directly: build the ask as an investment memo, with the expected return and the scenario where it doesn't land, rather than leaning on last year's ROAS as the only justification.

That memo needs the right numbers attached to it, and most marketing dashboards are still built around the wrong ones. Revisit which [KPIs](/insights/marketing-kpis-that-actually-matter) actually move the budget conversation forward before you finalize the deck, and have a clear answer ready on [ROI](/insights/fractional-cmo-roi) for whatever gets funded. A founder or finance lead who sees the return modeled out, with the risk named plainly, approves faster than one handed a spreadsheet and a hopeful percentage.

None of this requires a bigger team or a longer planning cycle than you already run. It requires treating budget season as the one chance all year to question the mix instead of extending it.
