A marketing mix model usually arrives as a deck. It shows which channels drove revenue last year, a few response curves, and a recommended reallocation. Then budget season starts, the recommendation meets last year's spreadsheet, and the spreadsheet wins.

That gap is common. A Harvard Business Review and Google [report](https://www.mobilemarketingreads.com/marketers-push-to-operationalize-mmm-as-adoption-grows-but-execution-gaps-persist/) found only 22 percent of organisations call themselves very effective at turning MMM insights into action, while 42 percent say they are not very effective at all. With 2027 plans being locked in right now, the teams that close that gap will move money while everyone else is still presenting charts.

## Quick answer: how do you turn marketing mix model results into budget decisions?

- Plan from marginal ROI, the return on the next pound or euro, and treat historical ROI as background.
- Turn each channel's response curve into a spend range with a floor and a ceiling.
- Validate any large shift with a geo or holdout test before committing the full amount.
- Tie budget reviews to the model refresh cycle, quarterly at minimum.
- Give one named person the authority to act on the output between planning rounds.

## Why most MMM results never change a budget

MMM results stall because the output arrives in a format that does not match how budgets get decided. Models report historical contribution by channel, while budget owners need to know what happens if they move a specific amount next quarter. Without that translation step, the familiar spreadsheet stays in charge.

The same report points to the friction behind it. Nearly half of respondents cited data quality as their top technical problem, 46 percent named slow internal workflows as a major obstacle, and 72 percent said MMM insights stay inside a small team.

That last number explains a lot. When the only people who understand the model are the analysts who built it, every recommendation has to be re-argued from scratch in the budget meeting. If your team is still deciding whether a model is worth the effort, our guide to [data requirements](/insights/marketing-mix-modeling-data-requirements) covers what has to be true before the output deserves that trust.

## Read marginal ROI before average ROI

Marginal ROI tells you what the next unit of spend will return, and that is the number budget decisions need. Average ROI describes how a channel performed across everything you spent on it historically. A channel can look excellent on average while already sitting on the flat part of its curve.

Google's [Meridian documentation](https://developers.google.com/meridian/docs/basics/roi-mroi-response-curves) draws this distinction directly: ROI is a historical, channel-wide average, while marginal ROI is the return on the next dollar spent. The two can point in opposite directions.

We see this most often with paid search. Branded and high-intent search usually shows the best average return in the model, which makes it the natural place to add money. Its marginal return is often much lower, because the demand it captures is close to fully harvested at current spend.

The practical rule is simple. Rank channels by marginal ROI when deciding where the next tranche goes, and use average ROI only to explain past performance to stakeholders.

## Turn response curves into spend ranges

Response curves become usable when you convert them into a spend range per channel, a minimum below which you lose meaningful volume and a maximum beyond which each extra unit returns too little. Budget owners can work with ranges. A single "optimal" number invites argument and gets ignored.

Read the curve in three zones. The steep early section is where spend is still buying growth efficiently. The bend is where returns start flattening. The plateau is where extra spend mostly buys impressions the model cannot connect to revenue.

Set the floor near the start of the bend and the ceiling where marginal ROI drops below your threshold. That threshold should come from finance, usually the return you need to justify any marketing pound at all. Meridian's documentation describes the same idea as a flexible-budget scenario, finding the maximum spend per channel that still hits a target minimum marginal ROI.

Ranges also make the conversation with finance easier. Instead of defending one number, you are showing where the line between efficient and wasteful spend sits for each channel, which feeds straight into an [annual budget](/insights/2027-marketing-budget-planning) built on evidence.

## Validate the biggest moves with a test

Any shift large enough to hurt if the model is wrong should go through an incrementality test first. A geo split or a holdout group turns the model's estimate into an observed result, and that result is what earns the budget owner's confidence. Small shifts can go straight through; big ones need proof.

[Recast](https://getrecast.com/integrating-marketing-mix-modeling-into-decision-making-process/) makes the same recommendation: build confidence through lift tests and geo-market tests, especially for decisions that are politically sensitive or financially significant. The test also feeds back into the model, tightening its estimates for the next refresh.

A workable threshold for a smaller team is to test any move above 15 to 20 percent of a channel's budget. Below that, the risk is low enough to act on the model alone and watch the next refresh. This is where MMM and [attribution](/insights/attribution-vs-measurement) stop competing and start supporting each other.

## Build a decision cadence around the refresh

The model only changes budgets if budget reviews are scheduled around its refresh. A quarterly refresh with a standing reallocation meeting a week later turns the output into a routine input. Annual models that land after the plan is set become reference material and rarely shift a line.

The HBR and Google research found more than half of marketers now update their models at least quarterly. Some platforms go further and recommend weekly updates, which suits teams spending enough that small shifts matter.

For most owner-run businesses and scale-up teams, quarterly is the right balance. It gives enough new data for the model to move, and enough time between reviews for tests to finish. Put the date in the calendar before the first results arrive, so the review exists before anyone has a reason to delay it.

## Who should own the call

One person needs the authority to reallocate within agreed ranges between planning rounds. Without a named owner, every recommendation waits for the next all-hands budget meeting, and by then the data is stale. In a small team this is usually the head of marketing, with finance agreeing the ranges upfront.

The owner's job is narrow and clear. Move money within the agreed floor and ceiling, log each change with the model output that justified it, and bring anything outside the ranges to the quarterly review.

This setup also fixes the access problem. When the owner explains each change in plain terms, the model stops being a black box held by the analysts.
