Meta told advertisers at its October 2026 Advertising Week session that its Ads Manager assistant will soon create campaigns, update targeting, and change budgets from a single conversation. Google's own Ads Advisor already applies some changes directly inside the platform. Both rollouts are moving on each company's own schedule, and most small marketing teams have no process yet for deciding what to hand over.

## Quick answer: should you let AI manage your ad campaigns yet?

- Meta announced on October 6, 2026 that its Ads Manager assistant will soon create campaigns, update targeting, and change budgets through conversational prompts, with preview and approval controls still in testing.
- Google's Ads Advisor, live since December 2025, applies only approved changes such as targeting tweaks and extensions. It does not touch budgets.
- Marketer trust is lagging adoption. StackAdapt found over 90% of marketers already use AI in advertising, but only about half trust it to act autonomously.
- Low-stakes work (drafting creative, summarizing reports, flagging anomalies) is safe to hand over now. Budget and targeting changes need a logged approval step first.
- Treat Meta and Google separately. They are not at the same point in this rollout, so one blanket policy across both platforms is premature.

## What Meta Just Added to Ads Manager

Meta's assistant can already generate creative and suggest targeting changes when an advertiser asks in plain language. The part that moves this from a copilot to an agent, creating full campaigns and adjusting budgets on its own, is still in limited testing, and Meta has only said it is coming "soon."

The assistant also picks up more memory and context. [Meta says](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/meta-introduces-new-ai-powered-tools-at-ad-week-2026/832288/) it now factors in a team's recent history, seasonal patterns, and stated goals, so recommendations do not start from zero in every session. Scheduled tasks are in testing too, including recurring performance reports and budget-review alerts that fire without anyone asking for them.

Previews and approval controls are the part worth watching closely. Meta frames them as the mechanism that lets a team decide what to hand off and what to keep, but [Relevant Audience](https://www.relevantaudience.com/meta/meta-advertising-week-new-york-2026-ad-tools/) notes the announcement gives no detail yet on how that approval actually works in practice. Handing a full campaign to an assistant is a very different decision than letting it draft a report, and it is worth weighing against what [AI agents](/insights/ai-agents-marketing-small-teams) can handle at this stage of their development generally.

The same event produced a related but separate announcement. Meta and Sierra introduced the [Personal Agent Protocol](https://sierra.ai/blog/introducing-personal-agent-protocol), an open standard for how a consumer's own AI agent authenticates with a business and completes tasks like bookings or returns. It covers commerce and service interactions rather than ad management directly, but it signals the same direction: agents acting on both sides of a transaction, with businesses needing visibility into what each one is doing.

## What Google's Ads Advisor Actually Does

Google's Ads Advisor and Analytics Advisor went live for all English-language accounts in December 2025, built on Gemini and first previewed at Google Marketing Live. Ads Advisor applies approved changes such as targeting tweaks, sitelink extensions, and policy-compliant fixes. It stops short of adjusting budgets on its own, which is a meaningfully narrower scope than what Meta is now testing.

Analytics Advisor works the same way on the measurement side. It answers questions about traffic changes and recommends next steps, but [Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/google-launches-ai-powered-advisors-464571) confirms it does not change anything itself. Google built both tools as a conversational layer on top of the controls a team already has, rather than a replacement for the person running the account.

Adoption still concentrates on lower-stakes work. Basis Technologies data cited by [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/google-agentic-ai-ad-buying-part-of-new-industry-norm) shows 79.3% of marketing professionals use generative AI for ideation, but only 27.1% apply it to media buying strategy and 26.4% to generating ad variations. The gap between drafting and deciding has not closed yet, on either platform.

## Why Marketer Trust Is Lagging Adoption

Most marketing teams already use AI somewhere in their ad workflow, but far fewer trust it to act without a person checking first. [StackAdapt](https://www.theatdb.com/news/stackadapts-ai-delegation-gap-90-marketers-use-ai-but-only-half-trust-autonomous-ai) found that over 90% of marketers use AI in advertising, while only about half trust it to run autonomously. That trust gap, more than how advanced the technology gets, is what should set the pace of adoption.

A separate [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/ad-platforms-launch-agentic-ai-features--marketer-distrust-persists) analysis of Yahoo DSP's rollout found 55% of marketers trust agentic AI to plan and execute tasks, while one in five still distrust it outright. The same analysis found only 39% of US and UK marketing and sales professionals feel confident their departments use AI to drive revenue, with nearly half sitting on the fence about it.

Part of that hesitation is about more than control. Over half of marketers in an [Ascend2](https://www.emarketer.com/content/ad-platforms-launch-agentic-ai-features--marketer-distrust-persists) survey worry AI will flatten creativity and strip out the human touch that makes a brand recognizable. Confidence is building, but slowly enough that treating every new agentic feature as instantly production ready would be a mistake most teams end up regretting within a quarter.

The safest deployments so far look like the ones we described in [self-adjusting campaigns](/insights/self-adjusting-campaign-automation): automation that reacts to signals within boundaries a person set in advance. That pattern holds whether the trigger is a behavioral signal in your own ESP or a suggestion from a platform's built-in assistant.

## Where To Start With Agentic Features

Start with the tasks where a person still reviews the output before a customer sees it, and hold the budget and targeting decisions until an agent's track record is proven on your own account. The order below follows risk level, from safest to most sensitive, regardless of how impressive any single feature looks in a demo.

Creative drafts, performance summaries, and anomaly alerts are reasonable to turn on now. Route anything customer-facing through a [human checkpoint](/insights/ai-content-workflow-quality-control) before it publishes, the same checkpoint you would use for any AI-assisted copy.

Targeting changes sit in the middle. Let an assistant propose them, but keep manual approval until you have a month or more of its suggestions logged against independently verified account results.

Budget changes should wait. Meta's preview and approval controls are not fully documented yet, and Google's advisor does not touch budgets at all. If you want to test agentic budget control, do it on a capped account first, with a hard ceiling the agent cannot exceed regardless of what it recommends.

Reporting questions are the easiest call. Conversational tools built like Google's Analytics Advisor slot naturally into the [AI reporting](/insights/ai-marketing-reporting) work a team already does, since they only answer questions rather than act on an account.

None of this requires a six-month AI policy document before your team can move. It requires deciding, platform by platform and feature by feature, what you are comfortable logging after the fact and what needs a person's sign-off first.
